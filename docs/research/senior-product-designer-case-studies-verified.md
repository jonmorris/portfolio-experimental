# Senior product designer case studies: source verification

Checked 26 November 2026. Every page below was opened and read in full unless the status column says otherwise.

**How to read this document.** Direct quotation is limited to one short excerpt per source. Everything else is a paraphrase with a link, so you can check the wording yourself. Text marked **Synthesis** is my reading, not something a source says.

## 1. Summary of conclusions

- **The earlier research pass holds up on principles and fails on numbers.** Trade-offs, a plainly stated role, constraints, outcomes and NDA handling are all well supported by primary sources. The specific figures ("30 seconds", "two lines", "2–3 forks", "most common rejection trigger") are supported by none of them.
- **No source read gives a "30 seconds" review time backed by a method.** The only timing figure with a stated method is 55 seconds, from a vendor study of 16 hiring managers. "30 seconds" appears as one survey respondent's remark and in unsourced newsletters.
- **The primary sources disagree about process.** NN/g (2019) wants the full process shown. Meta (2019), Atlassian (2023) and HubSpot (2025) say process is over-shown and want design decisions instead. The Atlassian piece criticises the NN/g advice directly. For senior roles the newer, decision-focused position has more first-party support.
- **Seniority is described as scope, complexity, initiative and influence.** No source says constraint stories in particular "read senior".
- **Three listed sources add little.** The GitLab page has moved and no longer discusses portfolios. The NN/g job-application article only links to other portfolio articles. The NN/g video could not be played and has no transcript on the page.
- **Simon Pan's site is down.** The Uber case study was read from an archived copy dated 8 January 2024.

## 3. Findings by topic

### (a) What a case study should contain, and in what order

- **NN/g (Krause)** lists, in this order: the problem or hypothesis, your role and collaboration, how you reached the solution, how it solved the problem, challenges, effect on users and the business, and what you learned. The challenges item reads: "Challenges you faced, including design concepts that were ultimately not pursued". [Link](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/)
- **Leslie Yang (Part 1)** gives a presentation outline per case study: role, responsibilities and duration; user problem; business goals; users; research; the link from insights to decisions; iterations; visual design; impact; what you would do differently. [Link](https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in)
- **Meta (Garron E.)** describes that same outline as the format nearly every portfolio uses, and calls it dull to read. His preferred online format is a brief description covering problem, solution and outcome, paired with a prototype, with a link to the full write-up. [Link](https://www.meta.com/design-at-meta/blog/5-ways-to-improve-your-design-portfolio-today/)
- **Intercom (Colman)** says a minimal sample works if it shows what the project was, the problems it solved, your role and process, and what you accomplished. [Link](https://www.intercom.com/blog/hiring-designers-at-intercom)
- **HubSpot (Klocek)** says to assume reviewers already know the standard process, and to share only as much process as explains the problem, the constraints and why your approach was right. [Link](https://product.hubspot.com/blog/portfolio-reviews-done-right)
- **Atlassian (Parnell)** argues that process has crowded out design in portfolios, and that most of a review should be spent on the designs and the decisions behind them. He quotes the NN/g article as an example of the process-first advice he disagrees with. [Link](https://medium.com/designing-atlassian/how-focusing-on-process-is-ruining-your-design-portfolio-f90cfaade0fc)
- **Atlassian handbook** asks candidates to cover their approach, the core problems, time management, collaboration, success metrics and learnings, but briefly. [Link](https://www.atlassian.com/company/careers/resources/interviewing/design)
- **Google** asks for the end-to-end process with the user problem and final solution clearly stated, and says to write as if no one can ask you questions. [Link](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/ux_design_portfolio_tips_19.pdf)

### (b) Leading with the outcome

No listed source says "lead with the outcome" in those words. The closest statements:

- **Meta**: because reviewers may not finish reading, "convey the major points at the beginning". He also suggests fitting everything noteworthy above the fold.
- **Spotify (Rosie Ferris, Product Design Manager)**: "Entice them in with a single sentence about the project." [Link](https://spotify.design/article/ask-spotify-design-06)
- **Adobe (Matthew Carlson)**: start by explaining why the project mattered, what benefits it brought and who it affected. **Adobe (Shannon McCready)**: set the problem context up front and emphasise impact over a list of tasks. [Link](https://adobe.design/ideas/ask-adobe-design-how-can-applicants-stand-out-when-interviewing-for-design-jobs)
- **NN/g**: hiring managers rarely read a whole portfolio word for word, so it should be scannable.
- **Simon Pan** does not lead with results. His impact section is last.

### (c) Rejected alternatives and trade-offs

This is the best-supported topic.

- **HubSpot**: "What paths did you explore before taking the one that shipped?" The post also asks for a hard constraint that forced a compromise you can defend, battles you lost, and battles you chose not to fight.
- **Atlassian (Parnell)** lists the questions he wants answered in a review, including "What was the biggest trade-off you made?", along with what you are least proud of and what you compromised on for technical reasons.
- **NN/g**: a surveyed hiring manager said they want to know what is not in the design and why, as much as what is. The article also says to include candidate solutions that were never built and explain the thinking.
- **Adobe (Sudeep Chaudhuri)**: in portfolio reviews, raise the challenges and "the decisions and trade-offs you made".
- **Adobe (Snowden)**: tell the messy version, including what was cut, what did not go well, and the gap between what was designed and what shipped. [Link](https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-ace-a-design-leader-job-interview)
- **Meta**: asks how many iterations you went through and how you chose the final one.
- **Intercom**: the PM and engineering manager deep-dive covers working with constraints and weighing trade-offs.
- **Simon Pan** shows this in practice: a GPS-transparency concept he dropped after a third round of poor test results, and a location-description feature shipped in a simpler form than the one he envisioned.

### (d) Role, scope and constraints

- **Role.** NN/g, Google, Intercom, Yang, Spotify and Atlassian all ask for it. Atlassian's handbook: "be upfront about your specific contributions". Yang says to use "I" statements. Two of the eight Adobe managers (Kenji Arakawa, Brijhette Farmer) say it is often hard to tell a candidate's own contribution from the team's.
- **Scope.** Yang (Part 2) suggests a slide stating team size, other teams you coordinated with, the stakeholders you had to align, and where your area sits in the product. Snowden asks leaders to say how many people worked on the project and whose work they were responsible for.
- **Constraints.** A hiring manager in NN/g's survey asked for the problem, role, constraints and timeline. NN/g adds that managers want to see you work within real constraints, not an ideal world. HubSpot asks which technical or business constraints shaped the design and which you pushed back on. Yang lists working within design constraints among the things to talk through.
- **Simon Pan** states his role with dates, names the team, credits individual designs to colleagues, and says where his involvement stopped.

### (e) Metrics, and what to do without them

- **Intercom**: they will ask "What changed because of your work?" and how you determine success.
- **Yang (Part 1)**: include "project impact and metrics if possible".
- **Meta**: asks what the actual outcome was, whether you met the business metrics, and if not, why.
- **Spotify (Ferris)**: asks for results or learnings, either quantitative or qualitative.
- **NN/g**: its worked example uses a second round of usability testing as the evidence that the solution worked, with a sales figure as business impact.
- **NN/g careers report (p. 76)**: managers wanted work tied to customer and business outcomes, and asked what metrics were tracked before and after.
- **Figma** (new-graduate advice): if a project did not ship, describe what could have happened.
- **Adobe (Snowden)**: he often meets candidates for whom "the presentation ends when the project ships".
- **Simon Pan** gives four percentage changes, says the absolute values are withheld for confidentiality, and reports that one metric (contact rate) did not improve significantly.

### (f) NDA and confidential work

- **NN/g**: three options. Show process images in place of polished UI, redact or blur identifying information, or recreate the designs in a generic style. The 2025 NN/g article adds that you should clarify, when accepting an offer, how you may document future work.
- **van Schneider**: use a short note, describe the type of work and your role in general terms, or password-protect the case study with the client's agreement. His repeated instruction is to "always ask your client for permission". [Link](https://vanschneider.com/make-portfolio-work-cant-shared)
- **Spotify (Daniela Estevez Fernandez)**: if case studies are password-protected, send the password with the application.
- **Simon Pan** opens with a short note saying that "I have omitted and obfuscated confidential information in this case study", then repeats a one-line omission note beside the data and metrics sections.

### (g) How many case studies, and how long

- **NN/g**: three to five, and adds that the number matters less than the variety shown.
- **Google**: "Include at least 3-5 case studies".
- **Meta**: three to five online. From his own audit (method not described) the average project ran over 1,300 words; he suggests cutting to 500, 200 or even 100. For a 30-minute review: about 15 minutes on one project, 7 on a second, 2 on a third.
- **Yang**: three to four case studies online, with three to five sentences per section. Two projects for a 45-minute presentation.
- **Spotify (Estevez Fernandez)**: three to four.
- **HubSpot**: one project in a 30-minute review.
- **Adobe (Snowden)**: one or two projects in a leadership review.
- **Atlassian**: a 10-minute case study in the hiring-manager interview, then a 45-minute portfolio review. Prefers work from the past three to four years.
- **van Schneider**: two or three curated projects is fine. On length: "Saying the same thing in 500 words takes effort." [Link](https://vanschneider.com/senior-portfolios-vs-junior-portfolios)

### (h) What distinguishes senior, lead or principal work

- **Yang (Part 2)** names four indicators: "initiative, product thinking, scope, and innovation". She ranks initiative and product thinking highest. Initiative includes writing or co-writing the brief instead of waiting for one.
- **HubSpot**: as seniority rises they expect projects that needed more advanced skill, influence and craft to land, shown through scope, scale and complexity, and told clearly to people who don't know the project.
- **NN/g careers report (p. 73)**: for senior hires, managers "also expect a strong body of work and evidence of experience".
- **Atlassian handbook**: senior ICs may also get a "triad" interview with product and engineering covering trade-offs and collaboration. IC reviews go deep on your choices; manager reviews focus on how you led.
- **GitLab**: staff and principal roles emphasise platform thinking, working without a dedicated PM, cross-team influence and comfort with ambiguity. This is interview emphasis, not portfolio guidance.
- **van Schneider**: senior designers curate, attend to detail and write concisely.
- **NN/g video**: the page summary says expectations are "different for junior vs. senior positions", but I could not watch it to learn how.

## 4. Claim verdicts

### Claim 1. Lead with problem + result in two lines; reviewers skim about 30 seconds — **Partly supported**

- **Supported:** put the main points first and keep it scannable (Meta, Spotify, NN/g, Adobe; see topic b).
- **Unsupported:** "two lines". Spotify says one sentence; Meta says a brief description.
- **Unsupported:** "30 seconds". None of the 15 listed sources gives any review-time figure.

Tracing "30 seconds":

- **[Presentum](https://presentum.io/design/hiring-explained/evaluating-portfolio-and-resume)** is the only figure with a stated method. Sixteen hiring managers reviewed 243 applications, and "it took them 55 seconds to make a decision". That time covers the cover letter and the portfolio together. No date, no named companies, average only. Presentum sells interview practice.
- **[UXfolio](https://uxfol.io/state-of-ux-portfolios-2026)** has "30 seconds" only as one respondent's remark, about finding the projects: "within the first 30 seconds, I reject it immediately". A LinkedIn summary by UXfolio's founder, seen only as a search snippet, says 65% of reviewers spend under four minutes on first open. I could not confirm that figure on the public report page.
- **[UX University newsletter](https://newsletter.uxuniversity.io/p/your-portfolio-is-why-youre-not-getting)** (course seller, weak, snippet only) says some managers spend 30 seconds, with no source.
- **The "6–8 seconds" figure** on SEO blogs is unsourced. [One of those blogs](https://mantlr.com/blog/death-of-design-portfolio-2026) says so itself.

**Synthesis:** "30 seconds" looks like practitioner folklore, not a finding. If you want a number, "about a minute, per one small vendor study" is the most you can say.

**Also untraced:** [one SEO blog](https://whennotesfly.com/work-skills/career-growth/how-to-build-a-ux-design-portfolio) cites an "NN/g 2024 hiring manager survey" with an 87% figure. I found no such survey in the NN/g material read here. The NN/g survey I could verify is the 2019 one, with 204 respondents who hire. Treat the 87% as unverified.

### Claim 2. Include 2–3 decision forks per case study, each naming the rejected option — **Partly supported**

- **Supported, strongly:** showing rejected options and trade-offs (NN/g, HubSpot, Atlassian, Adobe, Meta, Intercom; see topic c).
- **Unsupported:** the count. No source gives a number. HubSpot notes you cannot cover every such question in 30 minutes.

### Claim 3. State role plainly and name constraints; constraint stories read senior — **Partly supported**

- **Supported:** stating your role plainly (seven sources; see topic d).
- **Supported:** naming constraints (NN/g, HubSpot, Yang, Intercom).
- **Unsupported:** "constraint stories read senior". Sources tie seniority to scope, complexity, initiative and influence (topic h). NN/g treats handling constraints as basic to any designer and applies it to students too.

**Synthesis:** HubSpot's questions about constraints you pushed back on, and how you built support, are about influence. A constraint story reads senior when it shows that, not because it mentions a constraint.

### Claim 4. Show evidence even if directional; silence on outcomes is the most common rejection trigger — **First half supported; second half unsupported**

- **Supported:** directional or qualitative evidence is acceptable (Yang, Spotify, NN/g's usability-test example, Simon Pan's relative figures; see topic e).
- **Unsupported:** "most common rejection trigger". No source read ranks rejection reasons with missing outcomes on top.
- **Evidence pointing elsewhere:**
  - Meta's interviewer says the number one mistake he sees is too much emphasis on process.
  - Parnell makes the same complaint.
  - UXfolio's top four red flags, as reported in its founder's LinkedIn summary (snippet only), are low visual quality 55%, confusing structure 48%, obvious AI overuse 43%, and overly long content 42%. Missing outcomes is not among them.
  - Yang says she moves on from image-gallery portfolios because she cannot tell who did the work or why.
- **Evidence that it matters:**
  - Snowden says he often sees presentations stop at launch.
  - Intercom says impact is important to them.
  - A LinkedIn post attributed in search snippets to Dave Poore of StackAdapt lists as a non-negotiable: "Your case studies do not mention outcomes or impact". I saw this only in snippets and did not read the post.

### Claim 5. Keep a through-line across case studies — **Partly supported, weakly**

No source uses this idea as stated. Related advice:

- **NN/g**: decide the top three things a reader should take away about you, and use one template so projects look cohesive.
- **Spotify**: consistency of structure across projects (seen in a search snippet of the Ask Spotify Design article).
- **Yang**: show case studies of the work you want to be hired to do.
- **van Schneider**: curation gives a clearer picture of who you are.
- **Meta** pulls the other way: each of the three to five projects should highlight a different aspect of you.

**Synthesis:** the sources support coherence of selection and presentation. They do not support a single narrative thread running through every case study.

### Claim 6. Present NDA work with abstracted visuals and a one-line note — **Supported**

- NN/g recommends process images, redaction or blurring, and generic recreations.
- van Schneider recommends a short note and general description.
- Simon Pan's case study does both.

Two additions from the sources: get permission and read the NDA (van Schneider), and consider a password-protected version with the password supplied on application (van Schneider, Spotify).

## 6. Recommended case-study structure for a senior product designer

Grounded only in the sources above. The order is my synthesis; each element names its support.

1. **Opening, visible without scrolling.** One or two sentences on the problem, what you shipped and what changed, plus the strongest visual or a prototype. *(Meta, Spotify, Adobe)*
2. **Role and scope block.** What you owned, in the first person; team size; partner teams; stakeholders; dates; where your involvement ended. *(NN/g, Yang Part 2, Atlassian, Simon Pan)*
3. **Confidentiality note, if needed.** One or two lines, with visuals redacted or made generic. *(NN/g, van Schneider, Simon Pan)*
4. **Problem and why it mattered to users and the business.** Include how you knew it was real, and who chose to work on it. Say so if you framed it or co-wrote the brief. *(Meta, Yang Part 2, Intercom)*
5. **Just enough process to explain the constraints.** A sentence or a short paragraph, not a tour. *(HubSpot, Atlassian/Parnell, Meta)*
6. **The design decisions.** This is the main body. Show the designs at readable size. For the hard calls, give the options considered, what was rejected and why, the constraint behind any compromise, where you pushed back, and what you lost. *(HubSpot, Parnell, NN/g, Adobe)*
7. **Outcome.** State the metrics or qualitative evidence, how success was defined, and what did not move. Use relative figures if absolute ones are confidential. *(Intercom, Meta, Yang, Spotify, Simon Pan)*
8. **What you would do differently and what you learned.** *(HubSpot, Meta, NN/g, Yang)*

**Portfolio-level**

- Three to five case studies online, chosen for the work you want next. *(NN/g, Google, Meta, Yang, Spotify)*
- A short version of each on the page, with a longer write-up or deck behind it. *(Meta)*
- One or two prepared as presentations of 30 to 45 minutes. *(HubSpot, Yang, Adobe, Atlassian)*

**Open question the sources do not settle:** how long the online version should be. Meta argues for a few hundred words. Simon Pan's well-regarded case study is many times that. Meta's two-version approach is the only reconciliation any source offers.
