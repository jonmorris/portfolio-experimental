# Case studies for senior / lead / principal product designer roles: what the sources say

> **Superseded where they differ** by [`senior-product-designer-case-studies-verified.md`](senior-product-designer-case-studies-verified.md) (26 Nov 2026), which re-read the sources in full in a browser. This first pass worked from search excerpts only.

Research date: 2026-11-26. Question: what should a UX/product design case study contain, and how should it be structured, for someone applying to senior, lead or principal product designer roles in 2025–2026?

> **How this was researched, and its main limitation.** Direct page fetching was blocked in this
> environment (nngroup.com, handbook.gitlab.com, intercom.com and substack.com all failed DNS
> resolution through the session proxy). Every source below was read **through web-search result
> excerpts**, not as full pages. Quotes are the excerpt wording as returned. Where an author or date
> could not be confirmed, it is marked *(unconfirmed)*. Treat this as a well-sourced map of the
> evidence, and check the full pages before quoting them publicly.
>
> **Source tiers used below.** **[P]** primary: a company design team describing its own hiring, a
> named hiring manager describing their own screening, or NN/g research. **[S]** secondary:
> recruiter firms, books, practitioner essays. **[W]** weak: listicles, SEO and course-seller
> content, or anonymous posts. Synthesis that goes beyond what a source says is labelled
> **Synthesis**.

## Summary of conclusions

1. **Hiring teams evaluate reasoning more than artifacts.** Company design teams (HubSpot, Atlassian, Adobe, GitLab, Intercom) consistently ask for the problem, the constraints, *your* role, the decisions and trade-offs, and the result. Rejected alternatives and trade-offs are explicitly requested by HubSpot and Adobe [P].
2. **Lead with the outcome and problem, and keep the piece short and scannable.** Adobe design leaders say "start with the outcome", and NN/g says hiring managers rarely read portfolios word for word [P]. No primary source gives a word count. **The "30 seconds" figure has no traceable portfolio study behind it.** It most likely migrated from Ladders' eye-tracking study of *résumés*.
3. **Fewer, deeper case studies.** NN/g recommends 3–5 detailed case studies. Adobe and van Schneider favour fewer and deeper. In interviews, a single case study is usually presented for 10–30 minutes (Atlassian, GitLab, HubSpot) [P].
4. **Seniority is read from scope, initiative, influence and business or product thinking**, not from more process. Leslie Yang (Lyft) names four signals: initiative, product thinking, scope and innovation. HubSpot expects "more influence" from higher-level candidates. GitLab's Staff+ interview tests "strategic thinking" [P].
5. **Outcomes matter, but "silence on outcomes is the #1 rejection trigger" is unsourced.** NN/g says before/after screens are "not enough" without showing whether the work worked [P]. No survey with a stated method ranks rejection reasons. Process-heavy, overlong case studies are named just as often by hiring managers.
6. **NDA work:** NN/g recommends showing process, blurring identifying details, or recreating generic versions, and asking past clients what you may share [P]. Simon Pan's widely cited Uber case study opens with a one-line note that confidential details are omitted and obfuscated.

## Findings by topic

### 1. What a case study should contain

- **Problem, role, process, ideas that didn't make it, and the result.** The GitLab handbook (Product Designer hiring and job pages, current as of search) asks candidates to walk through a case study for about 30 minutes. It suggests "clearly outlined user and business problems and what your role was", the design process "including the sketches, wireframes, prototypes, or the ideas that didn't make it", and "the result of your work". It advises against sharing entire design files. [P] — GitLab, *Hiring Product Designers*, https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/product/ux/product-design/hiring-product-designers/ and *Product Designer* job family, https://handbook.gitlab.com/job-description-library/product/product-designer/
- **Approach, success metrics, collaboration, learnings.** Atlassian's *Design Interview Guide* says the portfolio review is 45 minutes: you present your best work, followed by hiring-manager Q&A. It suggests covering your approach and the problems solved, "time management, collaboration, success metrics, and learnings". It asks for recent work ("past 3-4 years") and warns against jumping between PDFs or sharing whole Figma files. Another Atlassian page describes a "10-minute case study, followed by 10 minutes of Q&A". [P] — Atlassian Careers, *Design Interview Guide*, undated (current page), https://www.atlassian.com/company/careers/resources/interviewing/design ; *How to nail your design interview*, https://www.atlassian.com/company/careers/resources/interviewing/how-to-nail-your-design-interview
- **Problem, constraints, why this approach, and rejected options.** HubSpot's portfolio review is a 30-minute deep dive on one project, "not a walkthrough of every stage of the design process". Interviewers want the problem, the constraints, why the chosen approach was right, and "contrasts with rejected solutions". They ask for a hard constraint that forced a defensible compromise, how technical or business needs shaped the design, how you pushed back and built support, and a decision you are not proud of. [P] — Stefan Klocek (HubSpot UX) *(author and date likely, not confirmed)*, *Portfolio Reviews Done Right: Tell the Story, Not Just the Process*, ~Feb 2025, https://product.hubspot.com/blog/portfolio-reviews-done-right
- **Challenges, decisions, trade-offs, lessons; credit the team.** Adobe Design managers ask for "challenges you've faced, how you identified and navigated them, the decisions and trade-offs you made, and what lessons you'll take". One director says "it's as important to acknowledge who you worked with as it is to clarify your unique contribution". [P] — Adobe Design, *Ask Adobe Design: How can applicants stand out when applying to design jobs?* (several named Adobe managers; undated, roughly 2022–2024), https://adobe.design/stories/our-people/ask-adobe-design-how-can-applicants-stand-out-when-applying-to-design-jobs ; and *…when interviewing for design jobs?*, https://adobe.design/ideas/ask-adobe-design-how-can-applicants-stand-out-when-interviewing-for-design-jobs
- **Strategy, problem definition, systems and collaboration, plus solid craft.** Intercom wants to see "how you influence product strategy, define the problem, understand the system, collaborate with others", as well as "modern, simple design work that shows the fundamentals of design". [P] — Jonathon Colman *(byline from aggregators, unconfirmed)*, Intercom, *What we look for when we hire designers at Intercom*, ~Mar 2020, https://www.intercom.com/blog/hiring-designers-at-intercom
- **Process plus evidence that it worked.** NN/g's article says a portfolio should cover "your entire UX process and not just the shiny artifacts". It is based on a survey of 204 UX hiring managers. On outcomes it gives a checkout example: did the redesign increase revenue? "Having before and after pictures of the checkout workflow is not enough." A surveyed manager is quoted: "Don't just show me the finished product. I want to see the messy process…" [P] — NN/g, *5 Steps to Creating a UX-Design Portfolio* (author and date not confirmed in excerpts), https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-design-portfolios/ ; survey source: Maria Rosala & Rachel Krause, NN/g, *User Experience Careers* 2nd ed. (2019 studies; 722 respondents, of whom 204 hire; only 49 left open-text comments on what they look for), https://media.nngroup.com/media/reports/free/UserExperienceCareers_2nd_Edition.pdf ; video: Krause & Rosala, *UX Portfolios: What Hiring Managers Look For*, Aug 2020, https://www.nngroup.com/videos/ux-portfolios-hiring/

**Synthesis:** across five company design teams the shared core is **problem (user + business) → your role and team → constraints → key decisions with alternatives and trade-offs → result and evidence → what you'd change**. None of them asks for a full chronological process tour, and HubSpot explicitly rules it out.

### 2. Structure and ordering: outcome-first vs. chronology

- **Lead with the outcome.** The Adobe.com design director says: "The most interesting takeaway in a case study is the outcome. Start there—don't bury the lede!" Another Adobe manager says case studies "should lead with a clear articulation of the problem facing the user or the business along with a summary of the impact", then key decisions and execution, with process sketches after. [P] — Adobe Design, *Ask Adobe Design … applying* (above).
- **Scannable, because nobody reads it all.** NN/g: "Very rarely will hiring managers take the time to read your entire portfolio word for word", so the portfolio "should be scannable and not contain unnecessary detail". [P] — NN/g portfolio guidance (excerpt via search; page cluster above).
- **Process overload is a named failure mode.** Mark Parnell, an Atlassian hiring manager, asks "how did we get to a place where process dominates nearly every design portfolio I see?" He says what he wants to see "is that the candidate can design", though the excerpt also still recommends documenting process visually. [P] — Mark Parnell, *How focusing on process is ruining your design portfolio*, Designing Atlassian (Medium), 9 Jan 2023, https://medium.com/designing-atlassian/how-focusing-on-process-is-ruining-your-design-portfolio-f90cfaade0fc . A design manager, Gabi Moore, named "very long case studies primarily focused on describing a process" as one of the most common patterns she sees. [S] — Gabi Moore, 2021, uxdesign.cc (via search excerpt; exact article not confirmed).
- **The minimalist position.** Intercom's Kostya Gorskiy argues the best portfolios are "simply a list of links to products the designer has worked on, along with screenshots and short, relevant descriptions". Each description should cover what the product is, the problem, your role if it was a team effort, and what is interesting. He says detailed process-photo case studies often convey little. [P, but 2017] — Kostya Gorskiy, Intercom, *The minimum viable product design portfolio*, 2017 (search results give both Apr and Aug 2017), https://www.intercom.com/blog/minimum-viable-product-design-portfolio
- **Elevator-pitch length.** Meta principal designer Federico Francioni advises a "succinct and robust" case study you can walk through "in no more than 5 mins". [S] — ADPList, *How to nail a portfolio interview*, 2022, https://adplist.org/blog/how-to-nail-a-portfolio-interview

**Where sources disagree:** NN/g's surveyed managers want "the messy process". Parnell, Moore, HubSpot and Intercom push back on process-dominated case studies. **Synthesis:** the two positions reconcile as *show process only where it explains a decision*. Process as evidence of judgment is wanted. Process as a ritual tour is not.

### 3. How many case studies

| Source | Says | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| NN/g, *5 Steps…* | "Choose 3–5 Projects as Detailed Case Studies" | P |
| Adobe Design manager | "fewer case studies with deeper process examples" beat a broad, shallow portfolio | P |
| Atlassian (*Portfolio reviews in the time of quarantine*, Designing Atlassian, ~2020, https://medium.com/designing-atlassian/portfolio-reviews-in-the-time-of-quarantine-c8fa7a933bdf) | put most relevant work first; reviewers "may only review one or two projects in the initial stages" | P (early-career framing) |
| Tobias van Schneider, *Senior portfolios vs junior portfolios*, https://vanschneider.com/senior-portfolios-vs-junior-portfolios | narrowing to "two or three strong projects" gives a clearer picture of who you are | S |
| Leslie Yang, *How to ensure your product design resume and portfolio get seen*, https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/how-to-ensure-your-product-design | first project should be your best: biggest scope, visual craft and product thinking | P |
| Interview formats (Atlassian 10+10 min; GitLab ~30 min; HubSpot 30 min, one project) | one case study presented in depth | P |
| UX Design Institute blog (course seller) | hiring managers "look for two to three" | W |
| Anonymous LinkedIn reposts | "2 to 4 strongest case studies" one click from home | W |

**Synthesis:** **3 strong case studies** (range 2–5), ordered by relevance and scope, with the first able to carry the application alone, is consistent with every source. Each should also work as a 10–30-minute live presentation.

### 4. Length

- **No primary source gives a word count.** The closest guidance: Intercom argues against a portfolio "that takes 30 minutes to read" [P, 2017]. An Adobe Senior Design Manager says "lengthy, dense case studies are harder for me to parse when under a time crunch" [P]. NN/g says scannable, no unnecessary detail [P]. Francioni says it should be walkable in about 5 minutes [S]. Exponent (a course seller) suggests asking whether it can be described "in 500 words or less" [W].
- **Time spent reviewing.** The "30 seconds" figure could not be traced to any portfolio study. Secondary sources trace the related "6 seconds" lore to Ladders' eye-tracking study of **résumés** (about 6 s in 2012, about 7.4 s in 2018). One writer says outright that they "could not find a published eye-tracking study of portfolio sites" [W, via search summary]. A uxdesign.cc author's informal survey of 100+ recruiters and hiring managers reported they spend "a few minutes", with one excerpt giving 54% of design managers at 5–10 minutes. The method is unpublished, so treat it as [W]. Matej Latin's *Only 30 seconds to reject your portfolio?* (uxdesign.cc / ADPList, 14 Mar 2025) is an opinion piece, not a study [S].

**Synthesis:** "short, front-loaded, scannable" is well supported. Any specific number (30 s, 60 s, 800–1,500 words) is not.

### 5. What distinguishes senior / lead / principal case studies

- **Initiative, product thinking, scope, innovation.** Leslie Yang (Lyft design leader) uses four seniority signals and weights them by team. On scope: "having design ownership of an app, surface area, or a major vertical". On product thinking: being "highly driven to validate how the experiences they create are received by the market". On innovation: one project that "push[es] the envelope", showing iterations and whether elements made it into scope. [P] — Leslie Yang, *What a hiring manager looks for in product design portfolios and presentations: How to show you're a senior designer (Part 2 of 2)*, Leading Product Design, ~Jun 2023, https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in-d25 ; Part 1 (balance of process, craft, user *and* business understanding): https://leslieyang.substack.com/p/what-a-hiring-manager-looks-for-in
- **Influence and clear explanation.** HubSpot expects higher-level candidates "to show more influence and craft", and to explain complex work clearly to people unfamiliar with it. [P] — HubSpot (above).
- **IC depth vs. leadership.** Atlassian's guide: for IC candidates, "a deep dive into the details of the choices you made". For management candidates, "how you led the team and shaped the final result". Senior roles may add a "triad interview" with Engineering and Product on "how you approach trade-offs". [P] — Atlassian (above).
- **Strategic thinking and business impact.** GitLab's Staff+ interview "focuses on design expertise, strategic thinking, and ability to work at a senior level". The job family asks for strategic thinking "grounded in data and business impact". [P] — GitLab (above).
- **Level is judged from scope and impact.** Dan Shilov says hiring managers gauge level "based on scope, impact, and check that it matches the job requirements". Interviewers focus on outcomes: customer reception, impact on engineering, and which metrics moved. [S, paid book] — Dan Shilov, *Land Your Dream Design Job* (Holloway), *How Your Portfolio Will Be Evaluated*, https://www.holloway.com/g/land-your-dream-design-job/sections/how-your-portfolio-will-be-evaluated
- **For leaders, spell out the team.** An Adobe piece on leader interviews advises explaining "how many people worked on the project, what role types or skillsets you required, and whose work you were directly responsible for". [P] — Adobe Design, *How to ace a design leader job interview*, https://adobe.design/ideas/how-to-ace-a-design-leader-job-interview
- **Craft still counts at senior level.** Parnell (Atlassian) wants to see "that the candidate can design". Atlassian's follow-ups split into "Product Thinking" and "Craft Excellence". Micka Touillaud, screening 800+ applicants for a Senior Product Designer role at Feedly, looked first at visual quality ([S], reported second-hand by MentorCruise, https://mentorcruise.com/blog/your-portfolio-keeps-getting-rejected-how-to-diagnose-what-is-missing/).
- **Widely repeated but weakly sourced:** "at Staff level, at least one example where your work clearly moved a meaningful business metric", "messy, ambiguous problems, not tidy feature work", "end-to-end ownership… influence on roadmap and strategy". These come from LinkedIn posts reposted many times with no confirmable original author [W]. They are consistent with Yang, HubSpot and GitLab, but should not be cited as such.

**Synthesis:** a principal-level case study shows (a) a scope that matches the title (a product area, platform or system, not one feature); (b) work the designer *initiated* or reframed; (c) influence on product direction, roadmap and partners, including pushback; (d) explicit trade-offs under real constraints; (e) evidence of business or product effect; (f) leverage, meaning what others could do because of the work (systems, standards, team practices). This is still backed by visible craft. (f) is the least directly sourced: it is implied by "systems design" (Intercom) and "scope" (Yang) but not named by any primary source found.

### 6. Outcomes when there are no (or few) metrics

- **Primary sources ask for evidence that the work worked**: NN/g (before/after "is not enough"), Atlassian ("success metrics"), Yang ("validate how the experiences… are received by the market"), Shilov ("which metrics moved"; also customer reception and engineering impact).
- **But metrics are not universally required.** Meta's Federico Francioni says adding challenges and metrics is "optional, per some people" [S]. Intercom's minimal format doesn't mention metrics at all [P, 2017].
- **On "directional" evidence**, only weak sources give tactics: "describe direction, magnitude, and why it mattered" (LinkedIn reposts [W]). One practitioner suggests observed usability results (e.g. how many participants completed a flow unaided), reduced rework, faster team delivery, and quoted stakeholder reactions [W, LinkedIn]. NN/g's researcher-portfolio article suggests asking clients for a **testimonial** when the work itself can't be shown [P] — NN/g, *Portfolios for UX Researchers: Top 10 Recommendations*, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-researcher-portfolio/
- **Fabrication:** no source endorses invented numbers. None was found that discusses it explicitly. "Never fabricate" is a common-sense integrity rule, and also a practical one, because live interviews probe each claim (Glassdoor reports for Meta describe interviewers who "dug deep into… reasons for decisions"; anonymous, [W]).

**Synthesis:** use, in descending strength: shipped metrics against a baseline → directional or relative change ("roughly halved") → behavioural evidence from testing ("4 of 5 participants…") → operational effects (adoption of a system, fewer support tickets, faster delivery) → stakeholder testimony. Always say which kind it is. Then state what you would measure next.

### 7. Confidential / NDA work

- **NN/g:** instead of polished UI with company-specific information, "showcase your process for these projects" (sketches, black-and-white wireframes, workshop facilitation). "Blur out identifying information", with extra care for financial and medical apps. Recreate screens with different styling (time-consuming, but avoids identifying brand colours). Ask past clients what they are comfortable sharing. Reread the NDA and expect to redact; clarify portfolio rights when taking a new job. [P] — NN/g, *5 Steps…*, *Portfolios for UX Researchers*, *UX Layoff Crisis: A Guide for Resilience and Recovery* (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-layoff-guide/) and *How to Apply and Secure a UX Job* (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/apply-secure-ux-job/). The excerpts did not reliably show which of these pages each point comes from.
- **Exemplar:** Simon Pan's Uber pickup case study opens: "to comply with my non-disclosure agreement, I have omitted and obfuscated confidential information in this case study." [P, exemplar] — via Prototypr, *Perfecting the pickup*, https://prototypr.io/news/perfecting-the-pickup ; original http://simonpan.com/work/uber/
- **Ask permission first.** Tobias van Schneider says that for unreleased or NDA work, ask the client: sometimes "you're allowed to post a little preview". [S] — https://vanschneider.com/make-portfolio-work-cant-shared
- **Disputed: password protection.** Some practitioner guides recommend a password-protected section. Others warn it adds friction and that passwords get passed on. [W, multiple Medium/uxdesign.cc posts]

## Gaps vs. the current template (`docs/case-study-template.md`)

What the template already gets right, per sources: outcome-focused headline and standalone summary (Adobe, NN/g scannability); "Output over outcome" and "mechanical process dump" as named mistakes (NN/g, Parnell, HubSpot); captions saying *why* an image mattered (consistent with decisions-over-artifacts); not faking research or metrics on side projects.

Gaps and unsupported claims:

1. **The spine is chronological; the sources are decision-centred.** The nine sections (Problem → Goals → Research → Process → Solution → Outcomes) read as a timeline. HubSpot asks for "not a walkthrough of every stage", and the sources together centre on decisions and trade-offs. The template folds decisions into "Process & decisions" and has **no explicit slot for rejected alternatives and the trade-off made**, which HubSpot, Adobe, Atlassian and GitLab all ask for. A standalone "Research" section invites the process tour the sources warn against.
2. **Outcome is section 8, not up front.** The template says sections 1–3 and 8 must "carry the story alone", but places outcomes after the process. Adobe says to start with the outcome. The TL;DR could be required to state result plus problem.
3. **Unsourced numbers.** "Readers spend ~60s", "800–1,500 words" and "~30% imagery / 70% text" were not found in any primary source checked (NN/g, IxDF, UXfolio and learnui.design pages were not fetchable to confirm what they say). Sources support only "short, front-loaded, scannable". Consider labelling these as house heuristics rather than research findings.
4. **Role = job title only.** Frontmatter `role` holds a title ("Senior UX Designer"). Sources want **your contribution vs. the team's**: team size and composition, what you personally owned, and who else contributed (Adobe, Intercom, GitLab). Nothing in the template captures this.
5. **No constraints prompt beyond "Goals & constraints".** HubSpot's prompts (a hard constraint forcing a defensible compromise, a tension you navigated, pushback and how you built support) are not reflected.
6. **No seniority signals.** Nothing prompts for scope (area or surface owned), initiative (did you start or reframe it?), influence on roadmap and partners, or effects beyond the feature (systems, team practice). These are the main senior/principal differentiators (Yang, HubSpot, GitLab, Atlassian, Shilov).
7. **Outcomes guidance assumes metrics exist.** "Lead outcomes with real metrics against the baseline" gives no fallback. Add an evidence ladder (directional → behavioural → operational → testimony), labelled honestly, and "what I'd measure next".
8. **No NDA pattern.** No guidance for confidential work. NN/g's methods and Simon Pan's opening-line exemplar could become a small optional block.
9. **No reflection on what went wrong.** "Reflection" exists, but HubSpot specifically asks for a decision you are not proud of and what you'd change. The template could prompt for it.
10. **Portfolio-level guidance is missing.** The template doesn't say how many case studies to show (sources: about 3, range 2–5), which order (most relevant or biggest scope first: Atlassian, Yang), or recency (Atlassian: past 3–4 years). Nor does it note that each case study will likely be **presented live for 10–30 minutes**, so it should double as a talk track.
11. **Citations to verify.** The template cites NN/g, IxDF, UXfolio and learnui.design generically. Only NN/g could be partly verified here (via search excerpts). The ~60 s figure in particular is not supported by the NN/g material found.
