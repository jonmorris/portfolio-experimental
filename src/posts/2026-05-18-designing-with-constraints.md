There's a moment, every time I start a personal project, where I reach for a
monospace typeface and feel a little guilty about it. Isn't this a crutch? A
costume? A way to *look* technical without being it?

I've stopped feeling guilty. Here's why.

## Constraints are a feature

A monospace grid removes a decision: every character takes the same width, so
alignment stops being a negotiation. Columns line up for free. Tables don't need
fiddling. The rhythm of the page is decided before you type a word.

That sounds limiting. In practice it's liberating — the same way a fixed color
palette or an 8px spacing scale is. The constraint absorbs a class of small
decisions so you can spend your attention on the ones that matter.

## When *not* to go full mono

I'll say the unpopular thing: long-form reading is genuinely a little harder in
monospace. Proportional type exists for a reason. For a portfolio and a blog
that's mostly short-to-medium writing, the trade is worth it — the aesthetic
coherence pays for the slight reading cost. For a novel, I'd choose differently.

## The actual point

The font isn't the design. But picking a strong, opinionated constraint *early*
shapes everything after it — and a good constraint, chosen on purpose, does more
work than a blank canvas ever will.
